A no-hype checklist for comparing air fryer capacity

Quarts are the least honest number on the box. Here is what to check instead, and why the biggest number is often the wrong buy.

Counter Space editorial 5 min read No hands-on testing claimed

The short answer

Capacity is the first spec anyone compares and the one most likely to mislead. A quart is a volume, and volume tells you almost nothing about how much food actually gets crisp.

1. Ask whether the number is one chamber or several

This is the single biggest source of confusion. A "6-quart dual basket" and a "6-quart single basket" are not comparable, even though the number matches.

The Chefman dual-basket in this set is 6 quarts across two independent 3-quart drawers. The Chefman TurboFry is 8 quarts in one drawer. The second has more usable volume for a single batch; the first can cook two things at two temperatures. If you compare 6 to 8 without knowing the split, you have learned nothing.

Rule: always write capacity as "X quarts, N chambers." If a listing will not tell you the split, treat the number as unverified.

2. Discount the top third of any basket

Air frying works by moving hot air around the food. Food touching food does not brown; it releases steam and the surface stays soft. That means the practical capacity of any basket is meaningfully less than its stated volume — the usable load is the amount you can spread without stacking.

We are not going to put a percentage on that, because it depends on what you are cooking and we did not measure it. The mental correction is simply: assume you will fill it about two-thirds of the way and still get good results.

3. Convert quarts into portions you recognise

Volume is abstract. Portions are not. Before buying, ask what the basket holds in terms of the thing you actually cook — a whole chicken, four chicken thighs in one layer, two salmon fillets side by side, a pound of fries spread flat.

This is the check that catches most bad purchases. An 8-quart drawer sounds enormous until you realise a whole chicken does not fit in it and a 26-quart oven chamber does. Conversely, a 26-quart oven is mostly empty air when you cook two portions, and you paid for it in heat-up time and washing up.

4. Separate "feeds four" from "cooks for four at once"

Manufacturers describe capacity in servings. Servings assume batching, and batching assumes you are willing to cook in rounds and hold the first batch warm while the second runs.

If you are fine with two rounds, a small basket feeds a family. If you are not — and most people are not, at 7pm on a Wednesday — then "feeds four" is a claim about your patience, not the machine.

Decide which person you are before comparing numbers. It changes the answer more than the numbers do.

5. Add the accessories to the count

An oven-style unit's capacity is partly consumed by whatever is inside it. The Emeril Lagasse model ships with six cooking accessories, which is genuinely useful and also means a rack or a basket is occupying chamber volume during some cooks.

Ask: with the accessory I would actually use in place, how much room is left? That is your real capacity, and it is smaller than the headline figure.

6. Then subtract the counter

Capacity has a cost that is not measured in quarts. A bigger chamber means a bigger footprint, and the footprint is permanent in a way the capacity is not.

Bigger is only better if the space exists. In a small kitchen, a 6-quart dual basket that lives happily on the counter beats a 26-quart oven that has to be lifted out of a cupboard — because the one in the cupboard does not get used. Convenience is a capacity of its own.

The checklist, condensed

  • Write capacity as quarts plus chamber count.
  • Assume usable load is roughly two-thirds of stated volume.
  • Name the portions, not the quarts: whole bird, four thighs, two fillets.
  • Decide whether you will batch, before you trust a servings figure.
  • Subtract the accessories you will actually use.
  • Measure the counter. Then choose the biggest unit that fits there.

None of this requires a review score. It requires knowing what you cook.

Takeaways

  • Always read capacity as quarts plus number of chambers.
  • Stated volume overstates usable capacity, because food cannot be stacked.
  • Translate quarts into named portions before you compare.
  • "Feeds four" usually means "in two batches" — decide if that is acceptable.
