How hot should chicken be? A temperature guide that does not depend on a timer

The safe numbers, straight from the USDA chart, plus why colour is the worst thing you can judge chicken by.

Counter Space editorial 6 min read No hands-on testing claimed

The short answer

There is a table for this, it is published by the USDA, and it is short. The reason it is worth reading carefully is that the numbers are specific in ways most home cooks assume incorrectly — and because "it looks white" is not one of them.

Three numbers people get wrong

Chicken is 165°F. It is not 160, and it is not 170. Every part of poultry — breast, thigh, wing, ground, giblets, and stuffing — shares the same number. There is no lower threshold for white meat.

Whole steaks and roasts are 145°F, with a three-minute rest. The rest is part of the instruction, not a suggestion. It is also the number that gets rounded up to 160 out of habit, which is safe and unnecessary.

Ground meat is 160°F. Not 145. This is the one that catches people, because a steak and a patty come from the same animal. The reason is mechanical: grinding spreads surface bacteria through the interior. A whole steak only ever has bacteria on the outside.

Why colour is not evidence

The USDA is unambiguous: colour is not a reliable indicator that food is safe, and most pathogens are destroyed between 140°F and 165°F. A hamburger cooked to 160°F, as measured with a food thermometer, is safe regardless of colour. Some ground beef turns brown on the outside before it reaches a safe internal temperature — the browning is a surface reaction, not a thermometer.

The flip side is worth knowing too, because "use a thermometer" is often read as "cook everything to the minimum". Doneness and safety are different things. The USDA says it directly: consumers may cook food to higher internal temperatures to achieve a particular doneness, and a thermometer helps you hit that without overshooting. A chicken breast pulled at a higher temperature is not safer — it is just drier.

Is your thermometer telling the truth?

Before trusting a reading, check the thermometer itself. The USDA documents a test that takes two minutes and needs nothing but a glass of ice:

Fill a large glass with ice cubes. Add drinking water and stir well. Immerse the food thermometer stem a minimum of 2 inches into the mixture, touching neither the sides nor the bottom of the glass. Wait a minimum of 30 seconds and check the reading without removing the stem from the water. It should read 32 degrees F.

If it does not, the thermometer is out by more than the manufacturer claims, and every reading it gives you is wrong by that much. This matters more than it sounds: the ThermoMaven in this set is stated by its maker as accurate to ±0.5°F, and the CHEF iQ Sense is stated as accurate within 1°F across four internal sensors. Those are claims, not verified facts, and an ice bath is how you turn a claim into a fact about the device in your hand.

What this means for an air fryer

An air fryer cooks hot and fast, and fast cooking is where timing goes wrong. A drawer unit at 400°F can take a chicken thigh from raw to overcooked in a window you would have to time accurately. With a thermometer, you stop timing and start checking — and because an instant-read probe cannot stay in the food during the cook, the pattern is: check at an estimated time, adjust, check again.

The alternative is a leave-in probe designed for the heat, which reports the temperature while the lid stays shut. The CHEF iQ Sense is stated by its maker as heat resistant to 1,000°F and is named on the listing as working in an air fryer.

The one rule to remember

Temperature, not time, and not colour. Everything else on this page is detail. The chart is short on purpose — print it, or bookmark the source, and stop guessing.

Takeaways

  • All poultry, including breast and ground: 165°F. Whole steaks and roasts: 145°F plus a three-minute rest. Ground meat: 160°F.
  • Colour is not a safety indicator — surface browning happens before the interior is safe.
  • Test the thermometer in an ice bath: it should read 32°F. That converts a manufacturer's claim into a fact about your device.
  • Pull at a higher temperature for doneness, not for safety. Overcooking is a choice, not a safety requirement.
