Questions to ask before buying a multi-function kitchen appliance

Published Nov 20, 2026 · 8 min read · because "10-in-1" is a number, not a promise.

The short answer

Feature counts measure marketing ambition, not usefulness. Before buying any multi-function machine, get answers to six questions: which functions you'd use weekly, what's included, what cleanup those parts need, what the warranty covers, what the certifications actually claim, and what the machine demands from you (counter space, an app, an account).

1. The "10-in-1" trap

Multi-function labels count modes, and modes overlap: "reheat" and "warm" may be the same duty cycle with a different name. A machine advertised as 24-preset (like the Emeril 26QT in this lineup, per its listing) and a machine with 4 presets (the TurboFry, per its listing) may cover the same weeknight cooking — the difference is how much menu-navigation you do.

Count jobs, not modes: air-fry, roast/bake, reheat, dehydrate, and proof/dough are distinct jobs; "fries" and "chicken" are not.

2. Six questions, in order

  1. Which functions would I use weekly? Write them down. If the answer is "air fry and reheat," a simpler machine with better fundamentals beats a complex one.
  2. What's actually included? The Emeril listing includes six cooking accessories; the CHEF iQ includes three probes and a hub; some rivals sell trays and racks separately. Included ≠ useful — check whether the extras fit your cooking.
  3. What's the cleanup bill for each function? Rotisserie parts and crumb trays clean differently than dishwasher-safe baskets. See the cleanup guide.
  4. What does the warranty cover, and who provides it? Verified in this lineup: 1 year from Chefman on the TurboFry (listing bullet); 2-year assurance from CHEF iQ on the Sense (listing bullet). Others unverified in our table.
  5. What do the certification claims actually say? Listings here cite cETL (TurboFry), NIST & NSF (ThermoMaven), and a long standards list (CHEF iQ: NIST, NSF, ETL, FCC, UL and more). These are the seller's published claims. If a certification matters to your purchase, verify the listing still shows it at checkout — and know what it covers (safety, accuracy, or both).
  6. What does the machine demand from me? Counter space (see cleanup guide), an app and account (CHEF iQ is app-displayed per its listing), firmware patience, and eventually a battery. Convenience features are rented, not owned.

3. Worked example: the oven-style fryer

The Emeril 26QT advertises 24 one-touch presets, French doors, convection cooking, and six included accessories (listing; $149.99 observed, 4.3★ with 18,379 ratings). Running the six questions: the job list is broad (air fry, bake, roast, reheat, toast…); accessories are included; cleanup claim unverified; warranty unverified; no certification claim surfaced in extracted sections; demands are significant counter space. That's a strong fit for someone replacing a second oven — and pure clutter for someone who wants crispy fries fast.

4. Worked example: the smart thermometer

The CHEF iQ Sense Gen3 counts five sensors (four internal, one ambient), WiFi + Bluetooth monitoring, 1000°F heat-safe probes, up to 50 hours per charge, and app-guided cooking (listing; $119.99 for 3-probe variant observed, 4.2★ with 2,178 ratings). The question isn't "is it smart?" — it's "do I cook long, unattended items often enough to pay for remote monitoring?" If you mostly cook weeknight portions, the $16.99 ThermoMaven answers "is it done?" for a seventh of the price (listing, 4.6★ with 3,499 ratings).

5. The red-flag list

  • Function counts that dwarf the number of physical controls.
  • "All-in-one" claims with accessories sold separately and unpriced.
  • App-required hardware with no stated offline fallback.
  • Certification name-drops with no certifier named on the listing.
  • Capacity in quarts with no basket dimensions anywhere on the page.
  • Listings whose own title and bullets disagree — we track one such case as FLG-004.
