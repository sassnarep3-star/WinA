Questions to ask before buying a multi-function kitchen appliance

Every appliance claims ten jobs. The useful question is which two it does well, and whether those two are the jobs you have.

Counter Space editorial 6 min read No hands-on testing claimed

The short answer

A "10-in-1" appliance is not ten appliances. It is one appliance with ten buttons, and the buttons are not equally good.

1. Which function is the appliance actually built around?

Every multi-function appliance has a native shape. An oven with a fan is an oven first; the air-fry setting is a mode. A drawer with a fan is an air fryer first; the bake setting is a compromise.

This matters because the secondary functions inherit the limitations of the primary one. The Emeril Lagasse 26QT is a convection oven with 24 presets — as an oven it has real volume for a whole chicken or a sheet of vegetables. Its air-fry mode is genuinely useful, and it is still an oven: it will not heat up like a drawer does.

Ask which function the manufacturer leads with in the product name. That is usually the honest one.

2. Are the presets programming, or just labels?

Twenty-four one-touch presets sounds like twenty-four capabilities. Often it is one capability with twenty-four time-and-temperature pairs. That is fine and genuinely convenient — but it is convenience, not additional function.

The check: pick three presets you would use weekly and ask what each one actually sets. If the answer is "time and temperature," you are buying shortcuts. Shortcuts are worth money. They are not worth counting as features.

3. Does a second function require a second accessory?

If dehydrating needs a rack, and the rack is sold separately, the function is not in the box. If it ships with the accessory — as the Emeril model does, with six — the function is real.

Count accessories as part of the product, then count them again as part of the washing up. Both are true.

4. For measuring tools: is it a thermometer, or a monitoring system?

The two thermometers in this set are a good illustration, because they are often compared as if they were the same product. They are not.

The ThermoMaven instant-read is a probe with a screen. You insert it, you read a number, you remove it. One second to a reading is fast enough that you stop guessing. It requires you to be standing there.

The CHEF iQ Sense is a monitoring system. Three wireless probes, WiFi, an app. It reports while the lid stays shut, across a long cook. It requires you to not be standing there — and it requires an app, a network, and software that keeps working.

Neither is better. They answer different questions: "is this done right now?" versus "tell me when this is done, I am going inside."

5. What happens when the smart part stops being supported?

This is the question almost nobody asks before buying a connected appliance, and the one most likely to determine how long it stays useful.

A probe thermometer with a screen works until the battery dies. A WiFi thermometer with an app works until the manufacturer stops updating the app, the phone OS moves on, or the cloud service is retired. That is a real risk with any connected kitchen device, and no listing will tell you the support horizon.

Ask directly: how long will the app be supported, and does the device work without it? The published listing for the CHEF iQ Sense did not answer either question, and we could not verify the answer from an official source. Marked unverified in our register rather than assumed.

6. Are the accuracy claims checkable?

The ThermoMaven listing states ±0.5°F accuracy and describes the unit as NIST and NSF certified. Those are meaningful claims, and they are claims — we could not verify the certificates against a published certification record, so our register marks them as stated, not verified.

If accuracy matters to you — and for candy, sous-vide, or food safety it does — the checkable version is a calibration certificate or a stated tolerance you can test with an ice bath. A logo on a listing page is not that.

7. Would you use the second function this month?

The bluntest question and the most useful. Multi-function appliances are bought for the second function and used for the first.

If you would not dehydrate anything in the next thirty days, the dehydrate setting is decoration. That is not a reason to avoid the product — it is a reason to stop paying a premium for it. Buy the appliance whose primary function you need, and treat everything else as a bonus you may never open.

The short version

Find the native shape. Discount the presets to shortcuts. Check accessories are included and count them for washing. For anything connected, ask what happens when support ends. Distrust certification logos you cannot verify. And be honest about which function you will actually use before the first month is out.

Takeaways

  • Every multi-function appliance has a native shape; secondary modes inherit its limits.
  • Presets are usually time-and-temperature shortcuts, not extra capabilities.
  • A function that needs a separately sold accessory is not in the box.
  • For connected devices, ask what happens when the app stops being supported.
