Go-to-market

Marketing plan

How this is meant to reach people, what it costs, and which parts cannot honestly start until something else is true first.

This is not a growth-hacking document. It is the sequence this publication can actually execute, in the order the dependencies allow, with the things that are guesses labelled as guesses. Two constraints shape all of it. The site earns nothing until a click converts on Amazon, and no traffic source exists yet.

Positioning

The category is crowded with review sites that claim testing they did not do, and with AI-generated listicles that all say the same five things. Both are easy to spot, and readers have learned to distrust the whole category.

The defensible position is narrow and a little uncomfortable: a site that admits what it has not tested, and is useful anyway. The writing focuses on the decision — form factor, batching, counter cost, cleanup — because that is what can be written honestly from published specifications, and it is also what the generic listicles skip.

Against a site that says "we tested 40 air fryers," this loses. Against a site that says "the Ninja is the best air fryer for families," it wins, because it tells you when the Ninja is wrong for you.

Who it is for

Someone standing in a kitchen, measuring a gap on the counter with their hands, deciding between two appliances. They are not reading for entertainment and they do not care about the brand's story. They want to know whether the thing fits, whether it does the two jobs they need, and whether they will still use it in a year.

That reader arrives from a search query with a decision inside it, converts at a reasonable rate, and does not need to be convinced the category is interesting. It is the only audience a small site can realistically serve.

The content engine: question-shaped guides

Every guide is titled as a question someone would actually type, and answers it without withholding the answer for a click. Four exist. The pattern is what matters, because it is repeatable:

  • Name the real decision, not the product category.
  • Give the reader a way to decide for themselves, not just an answer.
  • Say when the recommendation is the wrong one.
  • Link to products only after the decision is clear.

The current four are hypotheses about what people want to know. None of them is backed by search volume data, because none exists yet. That is stated on the open questions page rather than disguised as research.

Sequence, because order matters

  1. Prove the links work. One click per product, confirmed in the Associates tracking report. Everything else is worthless if this fails.
  2. Turn on measurement. Deploy the collector so clicks are counted from the first visitor, not the hundredth.
  3. Get indexed. Verify Search Console, submit the sitemap, confirm pages are actually in the index.
  4. Publish on a cadence. A new guide every week for eight weeks. Each added to the sitemap and feed in the same commit.
  5. Read the query data. At week eight, replace the hypotheses with what Search Console says people searched.
  6. Then consider paid acquisition, an email list, or a second category.

Steps five and six are the ones people skip, which is why most affiliate sites are a pile of articles with no evidence about which one worked.

Traffic channels, ranked by honesty

Organic search. The only channel that compounds and the only one this site is built for. Slow: expect months before meaningful impressions, longer before clicks. Requires the sitemap, feed and canonical structure that are already in place, plus Search Console verification, which is not.

Direct and referral. Close to zero without a distribution channel. There is no social presence, no newsletter, no community. This is the site's biggest structural weakness and it is recorded as such.

Email. The right long-term answer, and premature right now: capturing addresses with nothing to send them is worse than not capturing them. Hold until a cadence exists.

Paid search. Held. Bidding on Amazon trademarks is prohibited, and a new site with no conversion data cannot tell whether an ad is profitable. Advertising before measurement is guessing with a budget.

Social. Not attempted. Would require a real person's voice and regular output, which is a different job from writing guides.

What we are allowed to do, and what we are not

These are not preferences. They come from the Associates Operating Agreement and the program policies, and breaching them risks losing the account entirely.

  • No bidding on "amazon" or any Amazon trademark, or misspellings of it.
  • No paid search that sends users directly to Amazon; ads must land on this site.
  • No price trackers, price alerts, or price comparison over time.
  • No star ratings or review text without API access.
  • No Amazon logos or Program Content.
  • No implying Amazon endorses this site.
  • Disclosure above the content on every page, not only in the footer.

Each of these is quoted with a source link on the source register.

What success looks like, without inventing numbers

No revenue forecast appears anywhere in this document, because a forecast here would be fiction. There is no traffic history, no conversion rate, no verified commission rate, and no enrolled-link proof. Four unknowns multiplied together do not produce a number worth printing.

What can be tracked, honestly and from day one: whether pages get indexed, whether anyone clicks through to Amazon, and whether the Associates report eventually shows orders. Those three are the real scoreboard, and the first two are measurable immediately once the collector is deployed.

The campaign ledger starts empty with its columns defined, so the first genuine report can be entered without redesigning anything. An empty honest ledger is a working instrument. A ledger with placeholder figures is a liability.
