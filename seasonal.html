Seasonal collection · November & December

Holiday cooking, planned around a second chamber

One oven, four dishes, a table that will not move. The fix is not a bigger oven — it is a smaller one on the counter, with its own clock.

Every holiday kitchen runs into the same wall: the roast owns the main oven for hours at one temperature, and everything else wants something different. Sides want high heat fast. Rolls want a final hot burst exactly when guests arrive. Reheating wants crisp back in minutes. The main oven cannot be in four places, and a written schedule only stretches so far.

A second, small chamber breaks the constraint instead of managing it. This collection gathers the seasonal decision in one place: the sequence to follow, the products that fit the two jobs a holiday kitchen actually creates, and the honest limits of what we know.

The plan

The sequence, in one screen

Work backwards from dinner. The roast keeps the oven; the counter chamber takes everything that wants hot, fast, small, or reheated.

  1. Claim the oven for the long, inflexible dish. The roast, the covered braise, anything slow by design. Its clock is the least movable.
  2. Give high-heat, single-layer sides to the counter chamber. Sprouts, potatoes, roasted vegetables — browned by moving air, held warm in a covered bowl while the rest finishes.
  3. Make the counter chamber the reheat station. Anything that finished early comes back crisp there, not in the oven you are fighting for.
  4. Reserve the final burst for the counter chamber too. Rolls, gratin top-ups, biscuit refreshes — the short, hot jobs that land exactly when the oven is full.
  5. Write the two clocks down the night before. Oven schedule on one line, counter schedule on the next. The most common holiday failure is scheduling, and paper is the cheapest fix.
