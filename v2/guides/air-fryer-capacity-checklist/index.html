A no-hype checklist for comparing air fryer capacity

Quarts are the least comparable number on the box. Eight questions that get you to a real figure.

This guide answers: How to read capacity claims. It is a working editorial title, not a claim about search demand.

“Six quarts” and “eight quarts” are printed on the same shelf, in the same type, as if they measured the same thing. They do not. Capacity claims in this category are one of the few places where a careful reader can be systematically misled without anyone lying.

Here is the checklist we would use, in order.

1. Is the number total or per zone?

The most important question, and the one most often skipped. A dual-basket fryer sold as 6 quarts holds two 3-quart baskets. A single-basket 8-quart fryer holds 8 quarts in one open chamber.

Which is bigger depends entirely on what you put in it. For a pile of fries, the 8 is bigger. For two different dishes at two different temperatures, the 6 does something the 8 cannot. Comparing 6 to 8 without asking this question is comparing two different measurements.

2. Quarts or litres — and which litre?

Six US quarts is about 5.68 litres. Some listings mix the two on the same page. The Chefman dual-basket listing titles itself 6 quart and then lists capacity as “6 liters” in its own spec table — a 5% discrepancy that is small in practice and telling in principle. It is on the flagged page as FLG-007.

If a listing gives you two units and they do not convert, treat the number as approximate and go by the physical dimensions instead.

3. Does the shape match the food?

A tall narrow basket and a wide flat one can hold the same volume and cook very differently. Crisping depends on surface area and airflow, not litres. A single layer of wings in a wide basket browns more evenly than a deep pile in a narrow one, even at identical volume.

This is why “how much does it hold” is a weaker question than “what is the largest flat thing that fits”.

4. What is the largest single item that fits?

Get a ruler out. A 12-inch pizza needs a 12-inch chamber. A whole chicken needs height plus a rotisserie spindle. The Emeril listing specifies both — 12-inch pizza, 6-pound chicken — which is unusually concrete and therefore unusually checkable.

Basket fryers rarely publish this, because the honest answer is usually “not much”. A 3-quart drawer is roughly the footprint of two chicken breasts laid flat.

5. What is the usable capacity after the rack?

Several basket fryers ship with a crisper plate or rack inside the drawer, which raises food off the bottom for airflow. That plate eats into usable height. Volume is measured to the rim of the drawer, not to the top of the plate.

No listing in this catalogue publishes a usable-capacity figure. That is a gap worth knowing about rather than a number worth inventing.

6. Does capacity scale with power?

Bigger chamber, same heating element, slower recovery. A very large cavity with an undersized element will brown unevenly when full. This is the one place where wattage matters — and it is also the field we could not confirm for two of the three air fryers, because the listings either omit it or contradict third-party sources. See FLG-005.

Rather than print a number we cannot source, we direct you to the rating label inside the door or on the base of the unit.

7. Does the number describe the box or the food?

Some listings quote gross volume including the housing. None of the three here does that as far as we can tell, but it is common in the wider category. If a machine's quoted capacity looks implausible next to its dimensions, multiply the dimensions out and sanity-check it: a 12 × 13 × 10 inch box is about 1,560 cubic inches, or roughly 27 quarts of external volume, before subtracting walls, element and housing.

8. Would you ever fill it?

An air fryer works by moving air around food. Overfill it and it becomes an oven with a bad fan. The honest usable capacity is usually around two-thirds of the quoted figure for good crisping.

That changes the arithmetic. An 8-quart machine filled to two-thirds cooks about as much as a 10-quart filled to half. And a dual-basket machine's two 3-quart zones, each filled sensibly, often beat one overfilled 8-quart chamber.

The checklist, condensed

  1. Total or per zone?
  2. Quarts or litres — and do they convert?
  3. Is the shape wide or tall?
  4. What is the largest single item that fits?
  5. What does the rack take out of it?
  6. Is there enough power behind it?
  7. Is this the box or the food?
  8. Will you actually fill it, or overfill it?

Eight questions, and none of them is “which number is bigger”. That is the whole point.
