Questions to ask before buying a multi-function kitchen appliance

Multi-function usually means one function done well and five done adequately. How to tell which is which before you pay.

This guide answers: Whether the extra modes earn their space. It is a working editorial title, not a claim about search demand.

The Emeril French-door oven is sold with between 24 and 25 one-touch presets, depending on which line of its own listing you read. That number is the pitch: one box, many jobs. It is also the number that should make you suspicious, because a preset count tells you how many buttons exist, not how many modes work well.

These are the questions worth asking of any multi-function appliance, and of that one in particular.

1. Which function is the reason it exists?

Every multi-function appliance has a primary function it was designed around, and secondary functions that were added because the hardware was already there. A convection oven with a strong fan is primarily an oven that can air fry. An air fryer with a bake setting is primarily an air fryer.

Ask which one the manufacturer put the engineering into. If you cannot tell from the listing, the accessories are a clue: the Emeril ships with six, which suggests the oven cavity is the point.

2. How many functions do you actually need?

Count the ones you would use weekly. For most kitchens that is two or three: air fry, reheat, maybe toast. The remaining twenty-odd presets are a lookup table you will never open.

A preset is a shortcut to a time and temperature. Anything a preset does, you can do manually in two taps. Presets save seconds, not capability — with one exception worth noting: a preset that changes fan speed or element pattern cannot be replicated by setting time and temperature alone. If the listing does not say which presets do that, assume none do.

3. Does multi-function mean multi-compromise?

Sometimes. A single cavity optimised for air frying can underperform as a toaster, and a cavity optimised for baking can be a weak air fryer. The honest question is not “does it do six things” but “is the thing I care about the thing it does best”.

If air frying is the main event, a dedicated basket fryer will generally beat a multi-function oven at its own game. If oven cooking is the main event and air frying is a bonus, the multi-function unit wins easily.

4. What does the listing refuse to tell you?

Gaps are informative. The Emeril listing states no wattage and no temperature range. Those are two of the numbers you would most want for a 26-quart oven, and their absence is worth noticing.

We have left both blank on its product page rather than filling them from a secondary source. That is not us being pedantic: two of the air fryers in this catalogue have wattage figures that either cannot be found or are contradicted by other listings, and in a category where circuit load matters, a guessed number is worse than no number.

5. Can you verify how it is done, not just how long?

This is the question that separates a good multi-function purchase from a frustrating one. A preset runs for a time. It does not know whether your chicken has reached temperature.

That is why a thermometer belongs in this decision even though it is a separate product. A wireless probe like the CHEF iQ Sense — three probes, WiFi, rated to 1000°F — is for long cooks where you cannot stand at the oven: a roast, a rotisserie chicken, a long smoke. A one-second instant-read like the ThermoMaven is for the moment of checking: thin cuts, weeknight chicken, anything you are standing over.

They are not competitors. They answer the same question at different distances. For a multi-function oven that will be used for a 6-pound rotisserie chicken, the wireless probe is the one that matches the use case. For everything else, the instant-read is enough and costs far less.

6. What does it cost you in space and parts?

A multi-function appliance takes the footprint of its largest function and the cleaning burden of all of them. That is the structural trade: you get one box instead of three, and you wash the parts of the one box that you used.

Before buying, count the accessories and ask where they will live. Six included accessories with no drawer to put them in will end up in a bag in a cupboard, and then you will stop using the functions they enable.

7. Will the seller honour the warranty?

Worth checking on any appliance with moving parts and a heating element. Note that the Emeril listing is titled “Emeril Lagasse” but its seller block is labelled “Innovation IQ” — the brand in the title and the seller on the page are not the same name. That is not unusual in licensed cookware, but it does mean you should confirm who services the warranty before you rely on it. It is logged on our flagged page.

The short version

Buy a multi-function appliance for the function you will use most, choose it on that function's merits, and treat the rest as free extras. Ignore the preset count entirely. And budget for a thermometer, because no preset in any of these machines knows whether your food is cooked.
